Week 2: Data Warehouses, Data Lakes, and Lakehouses Sep 15, 17

Key Questions

  • What are the main differences between operational and analytical infrastructure?
  • What are data warehouses? What problems did they evolve to solve?
  • What are data lakes and lakehouses? What problems did they evolve to solve?
  • What are the components of modern data platforms?
  • How do operational and analytical data models differ?
  • What goes on in ETL/ELT?
  • How do different physical representations of data affect storage, compute, and other tradeoffs within data platforms?

Readings

  • Designing Data-Intensive Applications, 2nd Edition:
    • Chapter 3. Data Models and Query Languages — skip "Event Sourcing and CQRS" but read everything else
    • Chapter 4. Storage and Retrieval — Intro section and "Data Storage for Analytics" (i.e., skip "Storage and Indexing for OLTP" and "Multidimensional and Full-Text Indexes")
    • Chapter 5. Encoding and Evolution — Intro section and "Formats for Encoding Data" (skip "Modes of Dataflow")
  • (Optional) Fundamentals of Data Engineering (These readings cover similar content but offer a complementary perspective):
    • Chapter 3. Designing Good Data Architecture — Discussion of data warehouses, ETL, etc.
    • Chapter 8. Queries, Modeling, and Transformation — Discussion of data modeling, star and snowflake organizations, etc.

The above readings are available for free online through the university's library. The links above point directly to Waterloo proxied content, but if you're having trouble accessing the content (e.g., due to VPN settings), you might have to go through the library's portal (i.e., search for the book title and follow the appropriate link).

  • What Goes Around Comes Around... And Around... by Michael Stonebraker and Andrew Pavlo (2024). Answers the question: Why relational?
  • What Goes Around Comes Around by Michael Stonebraker and Joseph M. Hellerstein (2005). Optional reading, but provides nice historical perspective, written over 20 years ago.

Week 3: Batch Processing I Sep 22, 24

Key Questions

  • What's the difference between scaling up and scaling out?
  • What are the implications of distributed processing across many machines?
  • What are the challenges for a divide-and-conquer strategy?
  • What challenges does partitioning address? What challenges does it exacerbate?
  • What challenges does replication address? What challenges does it exacerbate?
  • What's MapReduce and how does it work with HDFS?
  • What challenges do communication and skew present in scaling out?
  • Why is local aggregation important?

Readings

  • Designing Data-Intensive Applications, 2nd Edition:
    • Chapter 2. Defining Nonfunctional Requirements
    • Chapter 5. Encoding and Evolution — Intro section and "Formats for Encoding Data" (repeated from last week, ties in with 2nd lecture last week)
    • Chapter 6. Replication — Intro section and "Single-Leader Replication" (Hadoop uses single-leader replication)
    • Chapter 7. Sharding — Intro section to "Request Routing", inclusive (Hadoop uses option 2 in Figure 7-7; "Sharding of Key-Value Data" won't be directly relevant until next week, but makes sense to read together)
  • Hadoop: The Definitive Guide, 4th Edition:
    • Chapter 3. The Hadoop Distributed Filesystem
    • Chapter 7. How MapReduce Works

Although the Hadoop book is quite old, the overall design of the framework remains relevant. Focus on its overall architecture and the tradeoffs encoded in the design; you can safely ignore details such as specific APIs, command-line invocations, classes, etc.

The above readings are available for free online through the university's library. The links above point directly to Waterloo proxied content, but if you're having trouble accessing the content (e.g., due to VPN settings), you might have to go through the library's portal (i.e., search for the book title and follow the appropriate link).

The following are optional. They comprise some of the primary sources from which lecture content is drawn, and can enrich your understanding by providing broader context.

  • The Datacenter as a Computer: Designing Warehouse-Scale Machines, Fourth Edition by Luiz André Barroso, Urs Hölzle, Parthasarathy Ranganathan. (This is a long book, but worthwhile to flip through and read anything that catches your attention if you want a better understanding of datacenter design.)
  • The original GFS paper
  • The original MapReduce paper

In the original GFS/MapReduce papers, you'll notice an emphasis on aggregated storage (i.e., compute-disk locality). But that was over two decades ago... how does that approach compare to modern trends?

Week 4: Batch Processing II Sep 29, Oct 1

Key Questions

  • In what ways does Spark improve over MapReduce?
  • How is distributed group by implemented efficiently at scale?
  • How do commutative and associative operations contribute to efficient distributed execution?
  • How does partitioning contribute to efficient distributed execution?
  • How do these concepts come together in efficient joins at scale?

Readings

Reread the assigned readings from last week (or read for the first time if you haven't yet). The material will make a lot more sense given the lecture material. In addition:

  • High Performance Spark:
    • Chapter 2. How Spark Works
    • Chapter 4. Joins (SQL and Core)
  • Data-Intensive Text Processing with MapReduce:
    • Chapter 2. MapReduce Basics
    • Chapter 3. MapReduce Algorithm Design

Although both books are quite old, overall design, system architecture, and tradeoffs remain relevant. Focus on these aspects; you can safely ignore details such as specific APIs, command-line invocations, classes, etc. In the MapReduce book, Hadoop-specific material is outdated in the Spark context, but associativity and commutativity remain important as high-level concepts, independent of the execution framework.
